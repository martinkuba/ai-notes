# The rise of industrial software

**Original source:** [The rise of industrial software](https://chrisloy.dev/post/2025/12/30/the-rise-of-industrial-software)
**Author:** Chris Loy

## Summary

Chris Loy argues that software is undergoing an industrial revolution: AI coding tools are shifting software production from craft (costly, slow, expertise-dependent) toward manufacture (cheaper, faster, disconnected from human expertise). This creates a new class of "disposable software" -- created with no expectation of ownership, maintenance, or long-term understanding. Advocates call this "vibe-coded software"; skeptics call it "AI slop." Either way, its economics are fundamentally different from traditional software.

Loy invokes Jevons paradox -- the observation that increased efficiency in coal use led to higher overall consumption -- to predict that cheaper software production will drive explosively higher demand, not less. He draws parallels to other industrialized fields: printing gave rise to paperback genre fiction, industrialized agriculture produced ultraprocessed junk food, and smartphone cameras enabled user-generated video. In each case, industrialization created economic pressure toward excess low-quality output. Software may follow the same pattern, potentially reaching "social-media scale" for user-generated disposable apps. However, Loy argues traditional software won't disappear entirely, just as handcrafted clothing persists alongside fast fashion. The key difference is that software's intangible nature means innovation (solving new problems) drives value growth, while industrialization provides scale and commoditization. Together they create a cycle of progress. The open question is stewardship: as automated software production scales, technical debt becomes "the pollution of the digital world," and the hardest problem may be maintaining software that no one owns.

## Main Ideas

- AI is industrializing software production, shifting it from craft to manufacture and creating "disposable software"
- Jevons paradox predicts that cheaper software production will drive higher overall demand, not less
- Historical parallels: industrialization of printing, agriculture, and cameras each created floods of low-quality output alongside high-quality work
- Human-written software may become a boutique industry, like handcrafted clothing alongside fast fashion
- Innovation (solving new problems) and industrialization (scaling and commoditizing) drive progress in tandem
- The biggest unsolved problem is stewardship: technical debt is the pollution of the digital world, and no one may maintain the software that automated systems create

## Key Quotes

- "For most of its history, software has been closer to craft than manufacture: costly, slow, and dominated by the need for skills and experience."
- "Technical debt is the pollution of the digital world, invisible until it chokes the systems that depend on it."
- "Who maintains the software that no one owns?"
