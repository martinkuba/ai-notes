# The jobs AI won't take yet

**Original source:** [The jobs AI won't take yet](https://www.bbc.com/worklife/article/20230507-the-jobs-ai-wont-take-yet)
**Author:** Kate Morgan

## Summary

This BBC article examines which jobs are most insulated from AI automation, countering the narrative of wholesale job replacement with a nuanced view of how AI will change work rather than eliminate it. Goldman Sachs estimated that AI capable of content generation could automate a quarter of all human work, with 300 million jobs at risk across the EU and US. However, experts identify three categories of jobs that remain relatively safe in the foreseeable future.

Author Martin Ford identifies these three protected categories: genuinely creative work (not formulaic creativity but true innovation in science, law, and business strategy); jobs requiring sophisticated interpersonal relationships (nurses, business consultants, investigative journalists); and jobs requiring mobility, dexterity, and problem-solving in unpredictable physical environments (electricians, plumbers, welders). Notably, Ford warns that education level and pay grade are not defenses -- white-collar report writers are more threatened than Uber drivers or hotel room cleaners, because physical-world tasks remain much harder to automate. Labor economist Joanne Song McLaughlin adds that even in "safe" jobs, specific tasks will be automated while roles evolve to emphasize human skills. She uses bank tellers as an example: money counting was automated, but tellers now focus on customer connection and product recommendations, making social skills more valuable.

## Main Ideas

- Three job categories are most insulated from AI: genuinely creative roles, jobs requiring deep interpersonal relationships, and trades requiring physical mobility in unpredictable environments
- Education and salary are not defenses against AI -- white-collar workers may be more threatened than manual laborers
- AI is more likely to automate specific tasks within jobs rather than eliminate entire roles
- As routine tasks are automated, human skills like emotional intelligence and relationship-building become more valuable
- Physical-world jobs (plumbing, electrical work) are among the hardest to automate, requiring "a science fiction robot"
- Workers should identify their "complementary skill" -- the distinctly human ability that works alongside AI rather than competing with it
