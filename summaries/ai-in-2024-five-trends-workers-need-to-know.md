# AI in 2024: Five trends workers need to know

**Original source:** [AI in 2024: Five trends workers need to know](https://www.bbc.com/worklife/article/20240104-ai-in-2024-five-trends-workers-need-to-know)
**Author:** Leah Carroll

## Summary

Written in early 2024, this BBC article identifies five key trends shaping how AI would impact workers throughout the year. The piece takes an optimistic stance, arguing that after a year of generative AI being on employees' radars, workers are better positioned to understand and embrace the technology's role in the workplace. The five trends span inclusivity, hiring equity, diversity in development, upskilling, and regulatory challenges.

The first two trends focus on AI as an equalizer. AI-powered assistive technologies -- such as speech-to-text and real-time multilingual captioning -- are expected to break down barriers for workers with disabilities, with mainstream adoption benefiting everyone. In hiring and HR, AI is being developed to reduce algorithmic bias in screening tools, with organizations like the Hire Aspirations Institute at Harvard working to identify and mitigate bias. Companies like Deloitte are experimenting with AI to reallocate budgets and move workers to in-demand positions to stave off layoffs, which could particularly help marginalized groups who are disproportionately affected by corporate cuts.

The remaining trends address workforce adaptation and governance. Employers are expected to invest more heavily in upskilling programs, as a 2023 Jobs for the Future survey showed that 66% of Gen Z and millennial workers felt they needed new skills for an AI-driven workforce. However, the article acknowledges that AI regulation will continue to struggle to keep pace with the technology. While the EU AI Act and the US EEOC's Strategic Enforcement Plan represent steps forward, experts warn that a comprehensive regulatory framework will require global collaboration from corporations, governments, and academia. Workers concerned about AI's impact on their jobs report greater stress, anxiety, and burnout, underscoring the need for clear protections alongside technological advancement.

## Main Ideas

- AI-powered assistive technologies for workers with disabilities are expected to become more widely available, with benefits extending to all workers through tools like real-time multilingual captioning.
- AI is being developed to reduce bias in hiring processes, though the technology is far from perfect and cannot fully eliminate human bias.
- Inclusive development teams building AI tools are critical for heading off exclusionary practices from the start of the design process.
- Workers increasingly recognize the need for AI-related upskilling, with younger workers feeling particular pressure to update their skills.
- AI regulation continues to lag behind technological advances, requiring global collaboration to create a flexible and comprehensive framework.
- Workers worried about AI's impact on their jobs experience measurably higher levels of stress, anxiety, and burnout.
