# 15 Times to use AI, and 5 Not to

**Original source:** [15 Times to use AI, and 5 Not to](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/15-times-to-use-ai-and-5-not-to)
**Author:** Ethan Mollick

## Summary

Ethan Mollick presents a practical framework for deciding when AI is genuinely useful and when it can be counterproductive, drawing on both research and personal experience. The 15 recommended use cases fall into recognizable patterns: tasks requiring quantity (brainstorming hundreds of ideas), work where the user has enough expertise to quickly evaluate AI output, summarization with low error stakes, translation between audiences or frames, overcoming creative blocks, and coding. Mollick also highlights using AI as a reading companion, for generating variance to curate from, simulating audience perspectives, entrepreneurial tasks where an imperfect partner beats no partner, and automating ritualistic work that no one reads.

The five scenarios to avoid are equally instructive. AI should not replace genuine learning and synthesis, since asking for a summary is fundamentally different from reading and thinking. High-accuracy tasks are risky because LLM hallucinations are plausible and hard to spot, and research shows users tend to stop checking. Users who do not yet understand AI failure modes (sycophancy, persuasive hallucination) are prone to misplaced trust. Tasks where the struggle itself builds understanding -- such as writing and academic work -- lose their value when shortcut by AI. Finally, AI has surprising blind spots alongside surprising strengths, and the boundary (the "Jagged Frontier") keeps shifting.

Mollick concludes that knowing when to use AI is a form of wisdom that requires holding paradoxes: AI is most helpful where you are already expert enough to catch its mistakes, yet least helpful for the deep work that made you an expert. The patterns will continue to evolve as capabilities improve.

## Main Ideas

- AI excels at generating quantity (ideas, variations, drafts) where more output increases the chance of finding something good.
- Expert judgment is essential for evaluating AI output -- the user must be able to spot errors quickly.
- Summarization, audience translation, and perspective simulation are strong LLM use cases with manageable risk.
- AI should not be used for genuine learning, high-accuracy requirements, or tasks where the struggle itself produces understanding.
- LLM hallucinations are dangerous precisely because they are plausible; users often "fall asleep at the wheel" and stop checking.
- The boundary between what AI can and cannot do well (the "Jagged Frontier") is constantly shifting and requires ongoing experimentation.
- Wisdom about AI use is paradoxical: it works best for tasks you could do yourself but should not waste time on, yet can harm learning when used to skip necessary effort.

## Key Quotes

- "Like any form of wisdom, using AI well requires holding opposing ideas in mind: it can be transformative yet must be approached with skepticism, powerful yet prone to subtle failures, essential for some tasks yet actively harmful for others."
- "AI is often most useful where we're already expert enough to spot its mistakes, yet least helpful in the deep work that made us experts in the first place."
