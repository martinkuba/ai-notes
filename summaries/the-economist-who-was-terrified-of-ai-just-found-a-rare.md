# The economist who was terrified of AI just found a rare reason for hope

**Original source:** [The economist who was terrified of AI just found a rare reason for hope](https://fortune.com/2026/04/19/alex-imas-human-jobs-ai-economy-chicago-economist-substack-doomsday-scenario/)
**Author:** Nick Lichtenberg

## Summary

Economist Alex Imas, initially concerned about AI's potential to devastate employment and collapse economic demand, has developed an optimistic framework for how the AI economy could function without catastrophic job losses. His argument, detailed in his widely-read Substack newsletter "Ghosts of Electricity," draws on structural change theory—the economic principle that when technology commoditizes one sector, labor reallocates toward new areas of value. Using the historical example of agriculture (which employed 40% of the American workforce in 1900 but under 2% today), Imas argues that AI will similarly trigger a shift toward what he calls the "relational sector"—jobs involving human connection, emotional labor, and personal relationships like nursing, teaching, therapy, and hospitality work.

Imas grounds his optimism in behavioral economics and the concept of "mimetic desire," showing that people value goods and services partly because others desire them and they remain scarce. As AI makes commodity production abundant and cheap, demand will shift toward inherently non-commodifiable human experiences and relationships. He notes that billionaires with unlimited resources still spend most of their time on podcasts, social media, and live performances—consuming and producing relational goods—rather than passively consuming automated content. The example of Starbucks, which reversed course on automation to emphasize human connection and handwritten cups, illustrates how companies are discovering that scarcity and human presence drive value.

However, Imas emphasizes that this positive outcome depends critically on transition speed. If AI automation advances faster than workers and institutions can retrain and adapt, his earlier doomsday scenario—where unemployment collapses demand and shrinks the economy—remains plausible. He remains concerned about those underestimating AI's capabilities and urges broader education about frontier AI models.

## Main Ideas

- **Structural reallocation theory**: Historical precedent shows that technological productivity improvements in one sector (agriculture) trigger labor migration to new high-value sectors rather than permanent unemployment, as people's rising incomes create demand for different goods and services.

- **The relational sector as growth engine**: Jobs inherently requiring human presence, emotional intelligence, and personal connection—nurses, teachers, therapists, childcare workers, hospitality staff—will become economically valuable as AI commoditizes routine work. These jobs already employ nearly 50 million Americans.

- **Income elasticity and behavioral preferences**: When people become wealthier, they don't just buy more of the same cheaper commodities; they demand fundamentally different goods with higher "income elasticity," particularly relational and experiential goods that demonstrate scarcity and human involvement.

- **Mimetic desire undermines AI commodities**: Research shows people's willingness to pay for goods roughly doubles when those goods are scarce or exclusive. AI-produced goods lose this premium because they're perceived as infinitely reproducible, creating natural market advantages for human-created and relational offerings.

- **Transition speed determines outcomes**: The difference between an optimistic relational-sector future and economic collapse hinges on whether AI automation outpaces institutional and labor market adaptation. Rapid disruption risks demand collapse despite eventual structural reallocation.

- **Underestimated AI capability among skeptics**: Many people dismissing AI disruption based on outdated chatbot models fundamentally misunderstand frontier AI's accelerating capabilities and should be educated accordingly.

- **Complex institutions require irreducible human judgment**: Real-world examples (like medical research coordination) demonstrate that crucial institutional functions—relationship management, communication across expert domains, navigating regulatory complexity—cannot be fully automated and will become more economically valuable.

## Key Quotes

"The economics of scarcity won't disappear, it'll just relocate."

"You could be alone on an island consuming all the movies, all the video games, all of technology, everything you want. But most of the time, these billionaires, they're on podcasts. They're out there on Twitter, interacting with people, they're going to performances, they're consuming relational goods, basically, or trying to provide relational goods, like the need for socialization to be around humans."

"The speed of change really matters, whether we get to this hopeful version versus the more worrisome one."
