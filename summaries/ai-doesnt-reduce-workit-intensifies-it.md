# AI Doesn't Reduce Work—It Intensifies It

**Original source:** [AI Doesn't Reduce Work—It Intensifies It](https://hbr.org/2026/02/ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it)
**Author:** Aruna Ranganathan, Xingqi Maggie Ye

## Summary

Based on an eight-month ethnographic study at a U.S. technology company with approximately 200 employees, researchers found that generative AI tools did not reduce workload as promised -- they consistently intensified it. The study, conducted through in-person observation, internal communication tracking, and over 40 in-depth interviews across engineering, product, design, research, and operations, revealed that employees voluntarily worked at a faster pace, took on broader tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked. The company did not mandate AI use but offered enterprise subscriptions to commercially available tools.

The researchers identified three primary forms of work intensification. First, "task expansion": AI filled knowledge gaps, causing workers to step into others' responsibilities -- product managers wrote code, researchers took on engineering tasks, and individuals absorbed work that previously justified additional headcount. This created knock-on effects as engineers spent more time reviewing AI-assisted work from less technical colleagues. Second, "blurred boundaries": AI reduced the friction of starting tasks so effectively that workers slipped small amounts of work into breaks, lunch, and evenings. The conversational style of prompting made this feel more like chatting than working, eroding recovery time. Third, "more multitasking": workers managed multiple active AI threads simultaneously, creating a feeling of productive momentum that masked continuous attention-switching and growing cognitive load.

These three dynamics created a self-reinforcing cycle: AI accelerated tasks, which raised speed expectations, which increased AI reliance, which widened task scope, which further expanded work volume. The authors warn that what appears as a productivity win in the short term can mask unsustainable workload creep, leading to cognitive fatigue, burnout, and impaired decision-making. They recommend organizations develop an "AI practice" -- intentional norms including structured pauses before major decisions, sequencing of work into coherent phases rather than continuous responsiveness, and protected time for human connection and social exchange to counter the individualizing effects of AI-mediated work.

## Main Ideas

- Generative AI tools consistently intensified work rather than reducing it, across all roles studied over eight months at a technology company.
- Task expansion occurred as AI lowered barriers to unfamiliar work, causing workers to absorb responsibilities that previously belonged to others or justified additional headcount.
- AI blurred work-life boundaries by reducing the friction of starting tasks, causing work to seep into breaks, evenings, and mornings without deliberate intention.
- Increased multitasking with AI created an illusion of productive momentum while actually generating cognitive overload and continuous attention-switching.
- A self-reinforcing cycle emerged: acceleration raised expectations, which increased AI reliance, which widened scope, which further expanded workload.
- Organizations need an intentional "AI practice" with structured pauses, sequenced work phases, and protected human connection time to prevent burnout and sustain quality.

## Key Quotes

- "You had thought that maybe, oh, because you could be more productive with AI, then you save some time, you can work less. But then really, you don't work less. You just work the same amount or even more." -- an engineer in the study
