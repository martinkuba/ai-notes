# AI companies will fail. We can salvage something from the wreckage | Cory Doctorow

**Original source:** [AI companies will fail. We can salvage something from the wreckage | Cory Doctorow](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/ng-interactive/2026/jan/18/tech-ai-bubble-burst-reverse-centaur)
**Author:** The Guardian

## Summary

Cory Doctorow argues that the AI boom is a financial bubble driven by monopolistic tech companies desperate to maintain their growth-stock valuations, not by genuine technological capability. He introduces the concept of "reverse centaurs" -- workers who serve as appendages to uncaring machines rather than being augmented by them -- as the real labor dynamic AI creates. While a "centaur" is a person enhanced by a machine (like a driver with a car), a reverse centaur is controlled and monitored by one (like an Amazon delivery driver surveilled by AI cameras). Doctorow contends that AI companies promise investors they can replace human workers, but AI cannot actually do most jobs -- it can only assist, which does not deliver the cost savings investors expect.

The article dissects the financial mechanics behind the AI bubble. Tech monopolies like Google, Meta, and Amazon have exhausted organic growth and must continuously hype new technologies (video, crypto, NFTs, metaverse, now AI) to maintain their inflated price-to-earnings ratios. When growth stalls, stock valuations collapse catastrophically, as Facebook experienced with its $240 billion single-day loss in 2022. The AI narrative serves primarily to keep markets believing in future growth, with the actual technological viability being secondary.

Doctorow also pushes back against two common responses to AI. First, he rejects expanded copyright as a remedy for artists, arguing that in an industry dominated by a handful of publishers, studios, and labels, new rights simply get extracted from workers through standard contracts -- "giving your bullied kid more lunch money." Instead, he points to the US Copyright Office's position that AI-generated works cannot be copyrighted as the more effective protection, since it forces companies to pay humans for copyrightable content. Second, he argues that when the bubble bursts, useful residue will remain: cheap GPUs, skilled statisticians, and open-source models running on commodity hardware that can do genuinely useful tasks like transcription, image description, and document summarization. The real fight, he concludes, should target the monopolistic structures and financial incentives driving the bubble, not the technology itself.

## Main Ideas

- AI is a financial bubble driven by monopolistic tech companies needing to maintain growth-stock valuations, following the same pattern as previous tech bubbles (video pivot, crypto, NFTs, metaverse).
- The concept of "reverse centaurs" describes workers reduced to serving as appendages and accountability sinks for AI systems, rather than being empowered by them.
- AI cannot replace workers as promised to investors; it can assist them, but that does not generate the cost savings the $13 trillion growth narrative requires.
- Expanding copyright to cover AI training is counterproductive for artists because media monopolies will simply extract those new rights through standard contracts.
- The US Copyright Office's position that AI-generated works are not copyrightable is a more effective protection, forcing companies to pay humans for copyrightable creative work.
- When the AI bubble bursts, useful residue will remain: cheap GPUs, open-source models, and practical tools for transcription, summarization, and image editing.
- The root cause to fight is monopolistic market structure and growth-stock financial incentives, not the technology itself.

## Key Quotes

- "There is no alternative" is a cheap rhetorical sleight. It's a demand dressed up as an observation. 'There is no alternative' means: 'Stop trying to think of an alternative.'"
- "The AI can't do your job, but an AI salesman can convince your boss to fire you and replace you with an AI that can't do your job."
- "Creative workers who cheer on lawsuits by the big studios and labels need to remember the first rule of class warfare: things that are good for your boss are rarely what's good for you."
