# Fraud and the false optimism of AI for science

**Original source:** [Fraud and the false optimism of AI for science](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2026/04/22/fraud-and-the-false-optimism-of-ai-for-science/)
**Author:** Jessica Hullman

## Summary

Jessica Hullman examines the ethical tensions surrounding AI-assisted scientific research, challenging the "optimism" of proponents who view automated science as inevitable and necessary progress. She contrasts two perspectives: the optimistic view that using AI is irresponsible not to employ given its capabilities (analogous to adopting computational methods), versus the pessimistic view that outsourcing scientific work constitutes fraud because it compromises expert judgment and scientific integrity.

Hullman identifies multiple dimensions of potential fraud in AI-assisted research: misattribution of ideas when AI generates significant intellectual contributions, extension of methods beyond a researcher's genuine expertise, and the broader misrepresentation of what science fundamentally is. She argues that the line between legitimate tool use and fraud depends heavily on the researcher's approach and oversight. If a scientist maintains deep involvement and critical judgment throughout—understanding and evaluating every decision made by AI systems—the work may retain scientific integrity. However, if AI usage is driven by competitive pressure and metrics obsession while minimizing human oversight, it becomes fraudulent.

Hullman concludes that much pro-AI discourse, despite framing itself as optimistic, actually reflects technological determinism and pessimism about human agency. She critiques the assumption that technology alone can solve systemic scientific problems, arguing that meaningful science depends on humans who find the work intrinsically worthwhile and maintain quality standards. The real risk is that widespread careless AI use will crowd out careful research and disincentivize scientists motivated by genuine understanding rather than output metrics.

## Main Ideas

- **Fraud exists on a spectrum**: Using AI to generate research differs from paper mills in degree but may constitute fraud if it represents work beyond the researcher's genuine expertise or understanding
- **Expert oversight determines legitimacy**: AI use is only defensible if the researcher maintains detailed knowledge of and control over all significant decisions the AI makes
- **Misattribution of ideas is a concern**: When AI generates hypotheses, methodological choices, or framing, presenting these as one's own novel contributions crosses into misattribution
- **Science has intrinsic human dimensions**: Beyond producing results, science serves to develop understanding in researchers, shape field direction through taste, and train students—roles that require human judgment
- **Technological determinism masks pessimism**: Pro-AI narratives claiming optimism often reflect fatalistic acceptance of automation rather than genuine confidence in human-directed scientific improvement
- **System-wide effects matter**: Widespread careless AI use threatens to devalue careful research and discourage intrinsically motivated scientists
- **The attitude behind use is determinative**: Whether AI-assisted research constitutes fraud ultimately depends on whether the researcher approaches it with genuine critical engagement or competitive corner-cutting

## Key Quotes

> "It doesn't require fake data and it doesn't require some cackling Snidely Whiplash attitude. It can be some schlub sitting at a computer terminal who wants to get his contract extended or get admitted to a Ph.D. program or whose adviser is pressuring him to get some publications . . . But it's fraudulent publication, not the same as bad research."

> "If you think the reasons to do science are fundamentally human–that as scientists we are concerned with producing understanding for ourselves just as much as we're concerned about improving things in the world–then you could argue that for science to be meaningful we have to be the ones coming up with the ideas and shaping them as we go."

> "Increasingly, a lot of the most enthusiastic pro-AI discourse (including for science) strikes me as nihilism masquerading as optimism."
