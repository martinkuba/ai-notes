# Canaries In The Coal Mine? Six Facts About The Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence

**Original source:** [Canaries In The Coal Mine? Six Facts About The Recent](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Canaries_BrynjolfssonChandarChen.pdf)
**Author:** Stanford Report

## Summary

This Stanford paper by Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen presents large-scale empirical evidence of AI's employment impact using high-frequency administrative payroll data from ADP, the largest payroll provider in the United States. The data covers millions of workers across tens of thousands of firms through July 2025. The central finding is that early-career workers (ages 22-25) in AI-exposed occupations like software development and customer service have experienced a 13% relative decline in employment since the widespread adoption of generative AI, even after controlling for firm-level shocks.

The paper presents six key facts. First, employment declines are concentrated among young workers in AI-exposed occupations while experienced workers in the same roles remain stable or growing. Second, overall employment continues to grow robustly, but young workers specifically show stagnation since late 2022. Third, declines occur in occupations where AI automates work but not where it augments human labor—a distinction measured using observed patterns of Claude queries. Fourth, results hold after controlling for firm-time effects, ruling out industry-level confounders. Fifth, adjustments appear primarily in employment rather than compensation, suggesting possible wage stickiness. Sixth, the findings are robust across alternative sample constructions and did not appear before late 2022, when LLMs became widely available.

The research provides some of the first rigorous, large-scale evidence that AI is beginning to have a disproportionate impact on entry-level workers. The automation-versus-augmentation distinction is particularly notable: jobs where AI primarily substitutes for human tasks are seeing employment declines, while jobs where AI mostly complements human work are not.

## Main Ideas

- Early-career workers (22-25) in AI-exposed occupations experienced a 13% relative employment decline since generative AI adoption
- Employment declines are concentrated in automating rather than augmenting AI applications
- More experienced workers in the same AI-exposed occupations saw stable or growing employment
- Effects appear primarily in employment rather than compensation, suggesting wage stickiness
- Results are robust to firm-level controls, ruling out industry shocks as the explanation
- The employment pattern emerged specifically around late 2022, coinciding with generative AI proliferation
- Young workers in less AI-exposed occupations show comparable employment growth to older workers
