# Thousands of CEOs just admitted AI had no impact on employment or productivity

**Original source:** [Thousands of CEOs just admitted AI had no impact on employment or productivity—and it has economists resurrecting a paradox from 40 years ago](https://fortune.com/2026/02/17/ai-productivity-paradox-ceo-study-robert-solow-information-technology-age/)
**Author:** Sasha Rogelberg

## Summary

This Fortune article examines emerging evidence that AI has not yet delivered on its productivity promises, drawing parallels to Robert Solow's 1987 productivity paradox ("You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics"). An NBER study of 6,000 executives across the US, UK, Germany, and Australia found that nearly 90% of firms reported no impact from AI on employment or productivity over the past three years. While two-thirds of executives reported using AI, actual usage amounted to only about 1.5 hours per week, and 25% of respondents weren't using AI at all.

The disconnect between AI hype and reality is stark: 374 S&P 500 companies mentioned AI in earnings calls (mostly positively), yet Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok observed "no signs of AI" in employment data, productivity data, inflation data, or profit margins outside the Magnificent Seven tech companies. Academic studies paint a contradictory picture — an MIT study found only a modest 0.5% productivity increase over the next decade, while a ManpowerGroup survey of 14,000 workers showed AI use increased 13% in 2025 but confidence in the technology plummeted 18%. IBM notably announced it would triple young hires, recognizing that automating entry-level work would endanger its leadership pipeline.

However, some economists see signs of a potential "J-curve" reversal. Erik Brynjolfsson observed that Q4 GDP was tracking up 3.7% despite declining job gains, suggesting a productivity surge. He attributed a 2.7% US productivity jump to a transition from AI investment to reaping benefits. The article notes the historical precedent: the IT boom of the 1970s-80s eventually produced a 1.5% productivity growth increase from 1995-2005. Whether AI follows a similar trajectory may depend on how companies implement the technology across sectors of the economy.

## Main Ideas

- An NBER study of 6,000 executives found nearly 90% of firms reported no AI impact on employment or productivity
- AI use among executives averages only 1.5 hours per week, with 25% not using it at all
- The pattern echoes Solow's 1987 productivity paradox — technology visible everywhere except in productivity statistics
- No signs of AI impact on profit margins or earnings outside the Magnificent Seven tech companies
- Worker confidence in AI's utility dropped 18% even as regular use increased 13%
- A potential "J-curve" pattern may emerge, similar to how IT eventually boosted productivity in the 1990s-2000s
- IBM's decision to triple young hires suggests automating entry-level work could damage leadership pipelines
