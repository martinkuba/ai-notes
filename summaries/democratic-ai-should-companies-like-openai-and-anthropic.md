# Democratic AI: Should companies like OpenAI and Anthropic get our permission?

**Original source:** [Democratic AI: Should companies like OpenAI and Anthropic get our permission? | Vox](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/377555/ai-chatgpt-openai-god)
**Author:** Sigal Samuel

## Summary

Sigal Samuel argues that AI companies building technology aimed at radically changing the world should seek democratic buy-in from the public, and systematically addresses three common objections to this position. She notes that Sam Altman has described AGI as "magic intelligence in the sky" that will "break capitalism" and is "probably the greatest threat to the continued existence of humanity"—yet a few tech CEOs are making these decisions unilaterally. Even Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark has expressed unease about how undemocratic the process is, writing "How much permission do AI developers need to get from society before irrevocably changing society?"

The three objections she tackles: First, "our use is our consent"—she argues that using ChatGPT doesn't constitute informed consent, since people are often coerced by professional pressure and unaware of true costs (like energy consumption and climate impacts). Even if someone consents to using narrow AI, that doesn't mean they consent to AGI. Second, "the public is too ignorant to tell innovators how to innovate"—she counters that the public needn't dictate technical minutiae but should shape the direction of policy, and that technologies posing existential risks (like nuclear weapons) have precedent for democratic oversight. Third, "it's impossible to curtail innovation anyway"—she cites human cloning, the Asilomar moratorium on recombinant DNA, and the Outer Space Treaty as examples of successfully constrained technologies.

The piece draws on the Roman proverb "What touches all should be decided by all" and positions the AI question alongside nuclear weapons, biological weapons, and interstellar communication as decisions that affect all of civilization and therefore warrant democratic deliberation.

## Main Ideas

- AI companies are building potentially world-changing technology without democratic buy-in from the public
- Using AI products does not constitute informed consent to the broader AGI project—most users are unaware of true costs and risks
- Polling shows most Americans do not want AGI, even though many enjoy narrow AI tools
- Technologies with existential implications (nuclear, biological weapons, cloning) have precedent for democratic oversight and constraint
- The "technological inevitability" argument is a myth—societies have successfully chosen not to build certain technologies
- Even AI company leaders like Anthropic's Jack Clark express unease about the undemocratic nature of AI development
