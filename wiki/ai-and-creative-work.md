# AI and Creative Work

How AI affects creative industries — quantity, quality, and economic welfare. The evidence so far is more nuanced than either optimistic ("AI democratizes creativity") or pessimistic ("AI floods markets with slop") framings.

## Books: The First Large-Scale Empirical Case

An NBER working paper (Reimers & Waldfogel, 2025) studied AI's impact on book publishing from 2022 to 2025 — one of the first rigorous empirical analyses of AI's effects on a creative market.

**Supply tripled**: New ebook releases grew from ~100,000 monthly titles in 2022 to over 300,000 in late 2025, tracking closely with LLM diffusion curves.

**Quality declined on average, but more moderately good books exist**: AI-detected books receive 61% fewer ratings and lower star ratings than human-authored books. However, the absolute number of moderately high-quality releases increased, especially outside the top 200 titles per category-month. The tail of creative content expanded.

**Author selection, not AI itself, explains most of the quality gap**: ~65% of the human-AI quality differential reflects low-quality, inexperienced authors adopting AI — not AI inherently degrading writing quality. The gap narrows from 2023 to 2025 as tools and authors both improve.

**Modest positive welfare effects**: Consumer surplus from books increased 0.44% in 2023, 3.26% in 2024, and 7.23% in 2025 — real but small relative to the 3x supply increase. The long tail delivers value, but most of it is modest.

**No displacement of incumbent authors**: Authors active before LLMs increased their output during the AI era rather than losing market share.

**Category concentration**: AI adoption concentrated in nonfiction (travel, self-help, technology), not fiction or literary genres.

See source: [AI And The Quantity And Quality Of Creative Products: Have LLMs Boosted Creation Of Valuable Books?](../summaries/ai-and-the-quantity-and-quality-of-creative-products-have.md)

## Key Pattern: Long Tail Expansion, Not Frontier Improvement

The book data suggests a general principle: AI primarily expands the *long tail* of creative content rather than raising the quality ceiling. More content, more consumer choice, modest welfare gains — but the top tier of human creativity remains distinct and unreplaced.

This contrasts with AI's effects in coding, where frontier capability is advancing rapidly ([Agentic Coding](agentic-coding.md)). Creative work appears to have a different structure: quality is harder to measure, author selection effects dominate, and reader trust (proxied by ratings engagement) favors human-authored work.

## Selective Adoption

The finding that low-quality and inexperienced authors disproportionately adopt AI mirrors patterns in other domains. In software engineering, [comprehension debt](ai-impact-on-software-engineering.md) is most acute among developers who delegate without understanding. AI amplifies existing skill differentials rather than flattening them.

## Connection to Broader Debates

The welfare analysis reframes the quality vs. quantity debate: the question isn't "is AI content good?" but "does more AI content serve readers?" The answer is modestly yes, even when average quality declines — because quality distribution and discovery matter more than averages.

This is relevant to the skeptical case covered in [AI Critical Perspectives](ai-critical-perspectives.md): the "slopocalypse" concern is partially validated (average quality down, AI books underperform), but the welfare analysis cuts against pure doom — consumer surplus increased.
